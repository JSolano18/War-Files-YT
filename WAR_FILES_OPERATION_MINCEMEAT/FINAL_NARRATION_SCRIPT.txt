[00:55]  At around nine thirty that same morning, a Spanish fisherman working the water near Huelva saw something floating in the swell. It was the body of a man in British uniform.

[01:06]  Spanish soldiers carried him to Huelva and handed him to a naval judge. The papers in his pockets identified him as Captain, Acting Major, William Martin of the Royal Marines. Attached to him by a leather-covered chain was an official briefcase.

[01:51]  Within days, the papers inside it would pass through Spanish hands and into the files of German intelligence. Within two weeks, Adolf Hitler himself would be weighing what they said.

[02:03]  So who was Major William Martin? Why was he carrying secrets? And why did British intelligence want the enemy to read them?

══ CHAPTER II — FORTRESS EUROPE ══

[02:26]  By the end of 1942, the war in the Mediterranean had begun to turn. Allied armies were driving the Axis out of North Africa, and planners in London and Washington were already asking the next question: where to strike at Europe.

[02:42]  A cross-Channel invasion of France was judged impossible before 1944. Winston Churchill wanted to use the armies in North Africa to attack what he called Europe's soft underbelly.

[02:56]  There were two obvious choices. The first was Sicily, at the toe of Italy, less than a hundred miles from Tunisia. Taking it would open the Mediterranean to Allied shipping, and offer a stepping stone into Italy itself. The second was Greece and the Balkans, where an invasion could threaten the oil, copper, chrome and bauxite that fed Germany's war industry.

[03:20]  In January 1943, at the Casablanca Conference, the Allies made their choice. The target would be Sicily. The invasion was code-named Operation Husky, and it would be launched no later than July.

[03:35]  But Sicily was the obvious choice. Anyone looking at a map could see it. Churchill is reputed to have said that everyone but a bloody fool would know it was Sicily. If German divisions were waiting on its beaches, the cost of the landings could be enormous.

[03:52]  The Allies needed Hitler to look somewhere else, and they knew where he was already looking. Hitler feared an attack in the Balkans. So the Allies built a wider deception, code-named Barclay, to feed that fear: a fictional Twelfth Army based in Cairo, dummy tanks in Syria, Greek interpreters recruited, Greek maps and currency stockpiled.

[04:14]  But dummy armies and false radio traffic could only do so much. What the planners needed was evidence the enemy would believe, because the enemy had found it for themselves.


══ CHAPTER III — A VERY STRANGE IDEA ══

[04:30]  The idea was not entirely new. In September 1939, a memo circulated by the Director of Naval Intelligence, Admiral John Godfrey, listed dozens of ways to deceive the enemy. Historians have noted that it bore the hallmarks of his personal assistant, a young officer named Ian Fleming, the future creator of James Bond.

[04:54]  Item number twenty-eight carried a warning in its title: a suggestion, not a very nice one. It proposed planting misleading papers on a corpse, to be found by the enemy.

[05:06]  In September 1942, an aircraft flying from Britain to Gibraltar crashed off Cádiz. Among the dead was a British courier carrying secret documents. His body washed up on the Spanish coast. When it was returned, his letter appeared unopened. But other evidence showed that material recovered in Spain was reaching the Germans.

[05:29]  A month later, an RAF officer seconded to MI5 put the idea on paper again. His name was Charles Cholmondeley, secretary of the Twenty Committee, the small secret group that ran Britain's double agents. He called his plan Trojan Horse.

[05:46]  The committee turned it down as unworkable, but saw potential in the idea. Because the plan had a naval connection, its chairman paired Cholmondeley with the committee's naval representative: Ewen Montagu.

[06:00]  Montagu was a barrister and King's Counsel who had volunteered when the war began. In Naval Intelligence, he ran the section that handled counter-espionage. Together, he and Cholmondeley set out to discover what a plan like this would actually require.

[06:17]  First, they consulted Britain's most famous pathologist, Sir Bernard Spilsbury. Could a body convince a Spanish doctor that a man had died at sea? Spilsbury's answer encouraged them. Victims of air crashes over water often died of shock rather than drowning. And in Spain, he advised, detailed post-mortems were rarely carried out unless the cause of death seemed important.

[06:41]  Then they needed the body itself: someone whose death would raise no questions, and whom no one would come forward to claim. Montagu turned to Bentley Purchase, a London coroner. Bodies were not in short supply in wartime London, Purchase told him. But every single one had to be accounted for.

[07:00]  On the 28th of January 1943, Purchase got in touch. He had found a body that might work.

[07:09]  A week later, Montagu and Cholmondeley submitted their plan to the Twenty Committee. It needed a name. Montagu took one from a list of available code words: Operation Mincemeat.


══ CHAPTER IV — THE MAN WHO NEVER WAS ══

[07:25]  The man in the mortuary had a name. For more than fifty years, almost no one knew it.

[07:31]  He was Glyndwr Michael, from Aberbargoed, a mining town in the valleys of South Wales. His father, a coal miner, took his own life when Glyndwr was fifteen. His mother died when he was thirty-one. He had worked odd jobs as a gardener and a labourer. Homeless, with no money and no family to turn to, he drifted to London.

[07:52]  In January 1943, he was found in an abandoned warehouse near King's Cross, seriously ill. He had eaten rat poison containing phosphorus, a paste smeared on crusts of bread. Whether he meant to end his life, or was simply starving, no one can say. He died in St Pancras Hospital. He was in his mid-thirties.

[08:14]  No relatives were found to claim him. Montagu would later say that the family had given permission. Historians have found no evidence that they did.

[08:25]  The man they invented to take his place was very different. Captain, Acting Major, William Martin, Royal Marines, attached to Combined Operations Headquarters. Martin was a common enough name that several real officers of about that rank shared it. As a Royal Marine, he came under the Admiralty, so any inquiry about his death would come back to Naval Intelligence. And an acting major was senior enough to carry secrets, but junior enough that no one would expect to know him.

[08:54]  Then they gave him a life.

[08:57]  In his pockets they placed what spies call pocket litter: the small, ordinary debris of a real person. A photograph of a fiancée named Pam. Two love letters from her. A receipt from a Bond Street jeweller for a diamond engagement ring, priced at fifty-three pounds, ten shillings and sixpence.

[09:17]  A stern letter from his father. A note from the family solicitor. A demand from Lloyds Bank about an overdraft of seventy-nine pounds, nineteen shillings and twopence. Theatre ticket stubs. A bill for four nights at the Naval and Military Club. Keys, cigarettes, stamps, a silver cross and a St Christopher medallion. Together, they traced a man's last week in London, from the 18th to the 24th of April.

[09:44]  Pam was not real either. The photograph was of an MI5 clerk named Jean Leslie.

[09:51]  The identity card was harder. Photographs of the dead man looked exactly like what they were. So the team searched for a living man who resembled him, and found one inside MI5: Captain Ronnie Reed, who agreed to be photographed in a Royal Marine uniform. Montagu then spent weeks rubbing the cards against his trousers, so they would look worn.

[10:13]  Even the uniform was broken in. Cholmondeley, who was about the same build, wore it until it looked lived in. Piece by piece, a man who had died almost unnoticed was being turned into an officer whose death the enemy was meant to notice.


══ CHAPTER V — INTO ENEMY HANDS ══

[10:33]  Now came the documents that mattered. The key letter was written by Lieutenant General Sir Archibald Nye, Vice Chief of the Imperial General Staff, to General Sir Harold Alexander, who commanded the Allied armies in Tunisia. After several drafts failed to sound natural, Nye was asked to write it himself.

[10:54]  It read like a private letter between two senior officers: gossip, grievances, and almost in passing, the plan. Allied forces, it suggested, would land in Greece, near Cape Araxos and Kalamata. Sicily was mentioned, but only as a cover target.

[11:12]  A second letter, from Lord Louis Mountbatten to Admiral Andrew Cunningham, introduced Major Martin as an expert in amphibious warfare. [11:36]  So the papers could not be overlooked, they went into a briefcase, attached to Martin by a leather-covered chain, the kind used by couriers, looped around the belt of his trench coat.

[11:48]  Then the place. Advised by the Admiralty's hydrographer on tides and currents, they chose the port of Huelva, on Spain's southern Atlantic coast. Spain was officially neutral. [13:30]  Seraph sailed on the 19th of April, and was bombed twice on the way south. On the 29th, she reached the waters off Huelva.

[13:39]  At four fifteen in the morning of April 30th, she surfaced. Jewell sent the crew below, leaving only his officers on deck. They opened the canister and lowered the body into the sea. Jewell read the thirty-ninth Psalm. Then he ordered the engines full astern, and the wash of the propellers pushed the body toward the shore.

[14:00]  He signalled the Admiralty: Mincemeat completed.

[14:08]  In Spain, the plan now depended on a chain of strangers. On May 1st, two Spanish doctors carried out a post-mortem in Huelva, with the British vice-consul present. As the heat of the day rose, Haselden suggested that the doctors might like to finish up and go to lunch. They agreed, and recorded the cause of death as asphyxiation through immersion in the sea.

[14:31]  The next day, Major William Martin was buried in Huelva's cemetery with full military honours.

[14:39]  The briefcase, though, stayed with the Spanish navy. Despite pressure from Clauss, it was not handed straight to the Germans. It went to the naval headquarters at San Fernando, near Cádiz, and on to Madrid.

[14:53]  In Madrid, a senior Abwehr officer, Karl-Erich Kühlenthal, asked Admiral Wilhelm Canaris, the head of German military intelligence, to intervene. [15:18]  The information was passed to the Germans on the 8th of May. Kühlenthal considered it so important that he took the documents to Germany himself.

[15:28]  On the 11th of May, the briefcase was returned to the British. In London, the letters were examined. The eyelash was gone. [15:47]  To keep the Germans from suspecting a trap, the British sent another message they knew would be intercepted, claiming the envelopes had not been opened.

[15:58]  Then came the confirmation that mattered most. At Bletchley Park, codebreakers decrypted a German message warning that the Allied invasion would come in the Balkans, with a feint toward the Dodecanese. A signal went to Churchill, then in the United States. Mincemeat, it said, had been swallowed rod, line and sinker by the right people.

[16:20]  On May 14th, Admiral Karl Dönitz met Hitler. According to Dönitz's own record, Hitler did not agree with Mussolini that Sicily was the most likely target. He believed the captured Anglo-Saxon order confirmed that the attacks would be aimed mainly at Sardinia and the Peloponnese.

[16:39]  Hitler ordered that Greece, Sardinia and Corsica be defended at all costs. The experienced 1st Panzer Division was sent from France to Greece. By the end of June, German strength on Sardinia had doubled, to about ten thousand men. German torpedo boats were moved from Sicily to the Greek islands.


══ CHAPTER VII — OPERATION HUSKY ══

[17:03]  On the 9th of July 1943, the Allies invaded Sicily, in what was then the largest amphibious assault of the war.

[17:13]  Intercepted German signals showed that, even four hours after the landings began, twenty-one aircraft were leaving Sicily to reinforce Sardinia. Weeks later, Hitler was still expecting a blow in the Balkans. In late July, he sent Field Marshal Erwin Rommel to Greece to prepare its defence.

[17:33]  The fighting on Sicily was hard. Around sixty-five thousand German troops held off a far larger Allied army long enough for many of them to escape to the Italian mainland. But the island fell in thirty-eight days.

[17:48]  According to the historian Ben Macintyre, the British had expected around ten thousand casualties in the first week; the real figure was about a seventh of that. The navy had prepared to lose as many as three hundred ships. It lost twelve. A campaign expected to take ninety days was over in thirty-eight.

[18:09]  In the middle of the campaign, Mussolini was voted down by his own Fascist Grand Council and removed from power.

[18:17]  So did Operation Mincemeat win the battle for Sicily? Historians are careful here. Michael Howard called it perhaps the most successful single deception operation of the entire war. But he also argued that Hitler's long-standing obsession with the Balkans mattered more than any single deception. Others point to Hitler's distrust of his Italian allies. Macintyre writes that its exact impact is impossible to calculate.

[18:44]  What can be said is this. At the moment it mattered, German attention, and German troops, were pointed in the wrong direction. And a dead man had helped to put them there.


══ CHAPTER VIII — THE FORGOTTEN MAN ══

[19:00]  After the war, the story leaked into fiction, first in a 1950 novel by the former cabinet minister Duff Cooper. British security decided the best answer was to tell a version of the truth. Montagu wrote The Man Who Never Was in 1953. It sold millions of copies and became a film.

[19:20]  But he never revealed who the dead man really was. He described him as a bit of a ne'er-do-well, and said that the only worthwhile thing he ever did, he did after his death.

[19:33]  In Huelva, a headstone stood over the grave of a man who had never existed. It named his invented parents, and ended with a line of Latin: it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country.

[19:46]  Then, in 1996, an amateur historian named Roger Morgan found the answer in Britain's Public Record Office. The body was Glyndwr Michael. In the late 1990s, a new line was added to the grave in Huelva.

[20:02]  Glyndwr Michael served as Major William Martin.

[20:07]  Some researchers have since argued that a different body was used, a sailor lost with the carrier HMS Dasher in 1943. The official records of the operation, and most historians, name Glyndwr Michael.

[20:22]  In Aberbargoed, the town he left behind, his name now stands on the war memorial, beneath a Welsh inscription: the man who never was.

[20:33]  He never wore a uniform in life. He never chose to serve. He could not have known what was done in his name. But in the spring of 1943, a forgotten man from the Welsh valleys carried one of the most consequential deceptions of the war across the sea.

[20:50]  The Germans believed in Major William Martin. History, at last, remembers Glyndwr Michael.
